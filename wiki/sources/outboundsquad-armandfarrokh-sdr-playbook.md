# Outbound Squad — Armand Farrokh: Building an Outbound Machine + Creative Pipeline Plays

**Author:** Armand Farrokh, co-founder of [[entities/30mpc|30 Minutes to President's Club (30MPC)]] (interviewed by [[entities/jason-bay|Jason Bay]], CEO of Outbound Squad)
**Show:** Outbound Squad
**Source:** transcript, no URL captured

## Core Thesis

[[entities/armand-farrokh|Armand Farrokh]] went from insurance sales to management consulting to a mid-market AE role at [[entities/carta|Carta]] (skipping SDR entirely), got promoted straight into SDR leadership after becoming Carta's #1 rep, then took [[entities/pave|Pave]] from $0 to $13M ARR in two years as Head/VP of Sales. This episode is a complete, sequenced playbook for turning around and running an SDR organization — who to hire (and why prior SDR experience is close to a red flag), how to interview for it, how to build daily culture that survives the grind, how to target accounts so reps aren't wasting activity, and a set of creative, marketing-adjacent and executive-network pipeline plays that go beyond cold call/cold email. It closes with two pieces of personal-leadership philosophy (a stress-management model and a "decide big things early" lesson from a real ARR-target miscalibration at Pave) that generalize well beyond SDR management specifically.

## Full Guide: Every Piece of Advice, By Topic

### 1. Career path — how an insurance-sales dropout became an SDR-leadership authority

Farrokh's first sales job was selling insurance at Northwestern Mutual, where he was a top-2%-nationally performer as a college intern — but a $20,000 clawback (a real financial consequence of insurance-sales structure) made him quit sales entirely for 2–3 years, during which he worked in management consulting (PwC, then a strategy house staffed by MBB alumni). He returned to sales at [[entities/carta|Carta]] (a cap-table-management fintech) as a mid-market AE — skipping the SDR step because his insurance and finance background was judged sufficient. Carta was mid-transition from inbound to outbound: team size had doubled but lead flow stayed flat, so average quota attainment on his 8-person pod was 40–50%, with only one rep hitting quota. Farrokh — unafraid of outbound because of his insurance-cold-outreach background — built a repeatable cold-call opener ("heard the name tossed around") plus a problem-first pitch, became the #1 rep within 6–9 months at 250% of quota, and was promoted directly from AE to Director of SDR / manager of managers (skipping frontline manager) when the standing SDR director was let go. He tripled outbound pipeline while doubling team size, then moved to Pave as Head of Sales, taking it from $0 to $13M ARR in two years and becoming VP of Sales.

### 2. Why move from a winning AE seat into SDR leadership

Farrokh is explicit this wasn't "a break from quota" — he found leadership meaningfully *more* stressful than being an individual contributor, not less: as an AE, once you've solved the motion, the game becomes setting your own next-best personal record; as a leader, every day is a new, externally-imposed puzzle (e.g., "the board wants us to go from $4M to $20M this year — what do I actually do"). The real driver was a personal trait: he gets bored quickly once he's mastered a motion (3–6 months in, the thrill fades), and what didn't get old was mentoring — watching a junior rep on the edge of a performance-improvement plan land their first real win and seeing what it did for them personally. He explicitly took a pay cut moving from a ~300%-of-quota AE role into SDR leadership and describes it as the most fulfilled he'd ever been professionally.

### 3. The SDR org turnaround: "Flip 2, Fire 2, Hire 2"

See [[concepts/sdr-org-turnaround-playbook]] for full detail. Core claim: SDR orgs are fast to diagnose and turn around (about 3 weeks — you can tell almost immediately whether the right people are booking meetings with the right prospects and whether AEs like the meetings) compared to enterprise sales orgs, where a wrong call might not be visible for 6–12 months of deal cycles. Three levers fix most underperforming SDR orgs: (1) the right people in the room, (2) a raised standard of expectations with a culture built around it, (3) minimum-viable, modern frameworks (not outdated "hustle guru" tactics). Applied concretely at Carta: inheriting a team of 20 across 3 sub-teams after the top performers had just been promoted out, leaving a mediocre middle, some underperformers, and some unproven "young guns" — he "flipped" 2 young guns without entrenched bad habits into the new standard (200 dials/week minimum, with him personally embedded alongside them), "fired" 2 people showing ongoing underperformance or active resistance to the new culture, and "hired" 2 new reps immediately.

### 4. Making fast personnel decisions as a new leader

Farrokh's advice runs directly counter to the instinct to avoid "ruffling feathers" when you're new: he came into an SDR team while inheriting hostility left over from the prior AE-culture dynamic (a hostile inbound-era culture where every deal he created as an AE got scrutinized and contested) and made a deliberate distinction — protect anyone working hard and coachable, but remove anyone apathetic, complaining, or dragging their feet on every new initiative quickly, because that behavior "burns teams down from the inside" and blocks the people who actually want to improve. He credits some of the conviction needed for fast, potentially unpopular decisions to an earlier stint coaching wrestling, where a degree of "uninformed," fist-on-the-table certainty about the right way to do something made it easier to act decisively before overthinking crept in.

### 5. Redesigning the SDR interview process — and why prior SDR experience is overrated

See [[concepts/sdr-hiring-signal]] for full detail. Farrokh's data point: of the top 5 SDRs he's ever hired, not one had prior SDR experience — and he believes the same holds for his top 10 best SDR hires/transitions overall. Named examples: Josh Weissman (ex-Hollywood scriptwriter, came in via referral), Armin Davouti (pre-law UCLA grad), Bonnie Sarge (an Andreessen Horowitz intern), a current hire named McKenna, and Alan Chen (Pave's top SDR ever, a career changer from a BD background at My First Million/The Hustle). The two profiles that reliably work: career changers who've already demonstrated an ability to learn, write, or communicate (teachers, scriptwriters), and fresh college grads who show proactivity through extracurriculars (athletes, strong internships, debate). The interview process itself is a 3-round funnel: (1) a phone/Zoom screen selecting for proactivity and communication rather than resume pedigree; (2) a mock cold call, sent in advance with a basic framework to prep against, testing both the ability to apply new information under ambiguity and — critically — coachability, via a run-critique-rerun structure; (3) an on-site "super round" combining a mock cold email (surfaces writing quality and AI-dependence), a targeting/research brain-teaser with the hiring manager, and a free-form peer interview with an existing, bought-in rep testing grit and cultural fit.

**How Alan Chen got hired**: he cold-emailed Farrokh with a Loom video that wasn't actually tailored to him specifically, but was constructed so well (tone, framing, personal story, a direct callout — "if you don't hang on for the next 60 seconds, look out because you'll get some pretty creative follow-up from me") that it read as personal. Farrokh's insight: Chen wasn't just pitching himself — he was demonstrating, live, the exact skill the job requires: personalization at scale that reads as genuine even when it's templated.

### 6. Testing coachability specifically, with a repeatable method

The mock-cold-call round always runs twice. After call one, the candidate self-critiques first ("what went well, what didn't"), then is given one or two specific pieces of feedback max, then re-runs the call. Farrokh's predictive claim: every candidate who showed no change between call one and call two was gone within three months on the job; every candidate who showed a drastic change went on to succeed. Jason Bay's team runs a parallel, formalized 1–3 coachability rubric in their own interview process: 1 = not coachable, doesn't ask for feedback; 2 = receptive to feedback but doesn't proactively ask for it; 3 = actively asks for feedback and demonstrably implements it.

### 7. What the on-site "super round" actually screens for

- **Mock cold email**: reveals writing mechanics (spelling, conciseness) directly — and Bay adds a specific modern red flag: a candidate who cannot write a passable email without leaning on AI/ChatGPT is a real concern, even at a company (like both of theirs) that's otherwise bullish on AI tools.
- **Targeting brain-teaser with the hiring manager**: tests account research and structured thinking under an unfamiliar prompt.
- **Peer "wildcard" interview**: run by an existing rep who's already bought into the new culture, with full license to improvise. One example given: a rep who tested grit via oddball scenario questions ("it's a hot day in Florida, you have 17 cups of hot cocoa — how do you sell them") to see how a candidate reacts under a scrappy, off-script challenge, plus a "beer test" — would you want to be stuck with this person after a hard day of selling — since existing reps often have insider insight into who will or won't mesh with the AE team that a hiring manager alone would miss.

### 8. Daily culture-building: the dial blitz and public recognition

See [[concepts/sdr-culture-building]] for full detail. Every day from 9:00–10:00 AM, Farrokh ran a "dial blitz": the entire SDR team (himself included) picked up the phone at the same moment, dialing publicly and loudly on the floor, framed as team-vs-team competition (e.g., SF office vs. Salt Lake office, or AE vs. SDR) tracked in a global, company-visible Slack channel. Every meeting booked during that hour got posted immediately with the company name, how it was booked, the meeting date, and the responsible AE tagged directly — turning AE attention into public cheerleading for SDR wins rather than the more typical AE/SDR antagonism, and making it socially awkward to be the one person not dialing or not posting.

**Personal practice for modeling confidence**: Farrokh describes deliberately cold-calling loudly and unapologetically on an open floor specifically to interrupt what he calls the negative confidence spiral — feeling insecure lowers your voice, gets you rejected, makes you look around self-consciously, and that visible insecurity gets sensed by the prospect on the call. His counter-move: consciously "get big," accept in advance that colleagues will hear you flop, and use full-volume repetition itself to build real confidence rather than retreating to a phone booth to hide the discomfort.

### 9. Elevating SDR status org-wide

At Carta, Farrokh used a weekly all-hands "Show and Tell" (in front of ~400 employees) to reframe how the rest of the company perceived the SDR function, which most non-sales employees viewed as low-status "spamming." He opened with a still image from *The Pursuit of Happyness* — the scene where Will Smith's character, after enduring relentless rejection, finally books the one meeting that changes everything — and told the company: "SDRs do that three-minute movie scene for eight hours a day, and they have to book 16 meetings a month, sixteen times over, all day, every day, and it sucks." Paired with hard before/after numbers (dials and booked meetings both trending sharply upward for the team), this visibly shifted the company's perception of the SDR org from "annoying" to "impressively hard and worth respecting."

### 10. The three "minimum viable" SDR skills, and why targeting matters more than copy or calling

See [[concepts/outbound-targeting-triggers]] for full detail. Farrokh reduces the entire SDR skill set to three things done well and repeatedly: pick the right accounts and people, write a good email, make a good cold call. His diagnostic claim: the large majority of prospecting failures trace back to poor targeting (wrong accounts or wrong personas), not weak copy or calling technique — and, separately, the highest-activity reps on every team he's coached are never the top performers, because past a certain minimum-viable activity level, additional volume becomes junk that dilutes quality rather than adding pipeline.

### 11. Teaching account research via "shuttle runs"

Rather than sending a new rep to work 10 accounts end-to-end (where they compound roughly 17 different mistakes — wrong account, wrong persona, wrong trigger, wrong personalization angle, weak call opener — before anyone catches any of them), Farrokh isolates the account-selection sub-skill first: "go find 5 accounts matching this criteria," review together which are genuinely good fits and which aren't, send the rep back to replace the weak ones, and only once they can reliably self-identify good accounts does training move on to contact selection, then email, then the call — a deliberate-practice method that isolates one skill at a time instead of training the whole motion simultaneously.

### 12. Firmographic-plus-trigger targeting (the "stale cap table" example)

A prospect can match every firmographic filter in an ICP (at Carta: Series A–F, 50–500 employees) and still be a bad target if it's not in an active buying window — Farrokh's example is a "stale cap table" account: flat headcount, no funding in three years, meaning no new option grants and no compliance pressure, so there's genuinely nothing to sell them yet. The fix is layering trigger events on top of firmographics before any outreach: recency of the last funding round, employee growth rate (double-digit growth specifically, in his framing), and company stage relative to when compliance audits typically start (Series C, in the cap-table context) — disqualifying accounts upfront this way is what isolates the roughly 10–15% of the addressable list that's genuinely in a live buying window right now.

### 13. Personalization at scale via ranked trigger templates ("First is Best")

Credited to Farrokh's 30MPC co-host, referred to in the transcript as "Mark Koslow" (likely Mark Kosoglow — the well-known former Outreach CRO and 30MPC co-host [medium-high confidence on the name]): you can't hand-personalize every single SMB-volume email, so instead rank your possible triggers by strength (e.g., #1 = the account just closed a funding round yesterday; #4 = the account is growing 20–30%), pre-build one message template per ranked trigger, and at send time it takes about 10 seconds to identify which trigger applies to a given account and drop in the matching template — "connecting the dots" between account-level and person-level signal and the message, without needing to write each email from scratch.

### 14. Marketing-supported ABM plays beyond cold call/cold email

See [[concepts/abm-account-based-pipeline-plays]] for full detail. At Carta, prospecting into VC funds (an enterprise, six-figure-deal motion) ran as a coordinated marketing + SDR play: pick a specific sector angle (e.g., a competitor-takedown angle, or funds inside a particular compliance window), have SDRs work a tight account list (~25 accounts) while marketing runs synchronized display ads carrying the same messaging, layer in ABM-sent physical goods (literal pies mailed to VCs, with copy like "Carta helps you track your slice of the pie"), and add over-the-top executive-to-executive outreach (the CEO messaging fund leaders directly). Reported breakthrough rate on a tight 20–25-account list: roughly 10% on an "okay" campaign, up to 20% on a genuinely great one.

### 15. The SDR leader as marketing-alignment "train traffic conductor"

Farrokh's framing of his own role in the marketing relationship: marketing's MQL scoring frequently doesn't reflect what actually produces booked meetings for SDRs, so it's the sales leader's job to reverse-engineer the last 20 actual booked meetings *with* marketing and rebuild scoring criteria around what's empirically working — rather than passively accepting whatever lead-scoring logic marketing hands over. In the Carta VC play above, he personally picked the account lists and ran the campaign, using marketing specifically for multi-channel "air cover" support rather than as the primary driver of targeting.

### 16. Executive and investor-network referral pipelines

See [[concepts/executive-referral-pipeline]] for full detail. At Pave (employee #9, with effectively zero internal HR-buyer network but strong CEO/investor relationships), Farrokh approached portfolio-operations teams at the company's VC investors (Andreessen Horowitz, Index, Y Combinator named specifically) with a fully pre-packaged ask: "I've scored your 250 portfolio companies, identified the top 50 fits, and here are the two specific people-leader contacts I need an intro to at each, with a tailored blurb already written for each" — then asked to work through 5–10 intros per week, held biweekly review meetings with the VC teams to track progress, treated every referral "like gold" (sending gifts to both the referrer and the new prospect), and made sure every intro was closed out, not left hanging. Separately, he ran an internal "Exec Shark Tank Week": mapped every first-degree LinkedIn connection of the CEO, CTO, General Counsel, and Chief People Officer, had SDRs clean the resulting spreadsheets and ghostwrite ready-to-send intro messages in each executive's voice, and had the executives simply click send — generating 10–20 high-value executive introductions per month from networks that would otherwise have gone untapped.

### 17. Event pipeline generation: pre-booking and floor hunting

See [[concepts/event-pipeline-pre-booking]] for full detail. Core claim: for major industry events (his examples: HR Transform, Dreamforce), the pipeline value is captured almost entirely *before* the event, not by staffing a booth passively during it. Two concrete tactics: (1) pre-book roughly 30 meetings ahead of time by having reps work their entire existing book and cross-book meetings for each other before flying out, tracking a weekly "Road to [Event]" booked-meeting count for the three months leading up to it; (2) "floor hunting" — befriend whoever is checking badges at the door, ask to photograph the attendee/guest list under the pretext of checking whether a friend is on it, run that photographed list through ChatGPT to filter for ICP-matching accounts, then target those specific people directly on the show floor.

### 18. Where AI actually helps in sales, and where it doesn't

Farrokh's hot take on AI sales coaches (in-call pop-ups nudging reps mid-call, e.g., "do a give-get" while negotiating with a CFO): "useless" — deal coaching requires 10–15 nuanced, context-dependent questions about the specific industry, deal motion, and seller instincts that current AI tools can't meaningfully evaluate in real time. By contrast, he's genuinely bullish on AI for deterministic, checklist-style filtering work — his example: checking 50 accounts against 5 specific trigger criteria in about 5 minutes with AI assistance, versus roughly 3 hours doing the same check by hand. The generalizable line he draws: AI is strong at "if-this-then-that" mechanical filtering, weak at nuanced real-time judgment calls.

### 19. Managing personal stress: the "Two Arrows" parable

Farrokh cites the Buddhist parable of the Two Arrows as his personal stress-management model: the first arrow is the actual event that causes real pain (losing a deal, an adverse market shift) — unavoidable. The second arrow is optional: the resentment, blame, and internal emotional spiraling layered on top of the first arrow by choice. His framing: pain is inevitable in the job, but the added suffering is not, and part of managing it is recognizing that staying in a hard role or a tough territory is an active choice you're continually making over the available alternatives — which restores a sense of agency that directly counters the feeling of being "out of control" that drives most work stress.

### 20. The single biggest leadership lesson: make big, hard decisions early

Advice Farrokh would give his past self as a first-time leader: make big, hard calls early rather than "dying by a thousand cuts." His concrete example: Pave grew from $0 to $13M ARR in two years, beating its own year-one $4M target by hitting $6M — genuinely excellent performance. But the company had set a $20M target during the 2021 ZIRP-era funding environment, and when the market corrected in 2022, that target was no longer realistic. Rather than immediately resetting the target to something achievable (he suggests ~$12M would have been realistic) and right-sizing headcount to match, leadership held onto the original $20M goal — meaning the team felt like failures every single week despite having genuinely doubled revenue in a down market, purely because the target itself hadn't been recalibrated in time.

## Notable Quotes

> "Every single time I've hired someone who didn't change anything [between mock call one and two], that person was gone in three months. Every single time there's a drastic change, that person goes for it."

> "They already think I'm an idiot anyway, so I'm just going to laugh my way to the bank."

> "Make big, hard decisions earlier so you don't die by a thousand cuts."
